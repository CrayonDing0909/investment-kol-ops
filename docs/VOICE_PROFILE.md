# Voice Profile

## Purpose

This file defines the default voice for internal investment articles.

The goal is not to sound polished by AI. The goal is to sound like a curious
engineer-investor explaining what they learned, where they are still unsure, and
how they are updating their market map.

## Core Voice

- First-person, but not self-centered: use "I currently think", "I would frame
  this as", and "what I still need to verify" when judgment is involved.
- Research-forward: show the learning path, not just the conclusion.
- Preserve the raw thought before polishing it. The first pass should sound like
  a research voice note, not a finished explainer.
- Clearly label unverified ideas as a hypothesis map, not as conclusions.
- Plain Chinese with light English terms when the market or technical term is
  already commonly used.
- Prefer specific caveats over generic disclaimers.
- Keep the posture humble: this is research and judgment-building, not signal
  selling.

## Raw Voice First

- Start with a low-polish version:
  "Recently I have been organizing the AI server supply-chain narrative. It feels
  like there are a few branches."
- Keep uncertainty words when they are part of the user's thinking:
  "I imagine", "feels like", "roughly", "probably closer to", "not verified yet".
- Do not over-translate the user's wording into polished research language:
  if the raw thought is "CPU probably comes from agent AI demand", do not rewrite
  it as "CPU is the system orchestration bottleneck of the agentic AI era".
- Improve order, sentence breaks, and clarity, but do not sand the voice down
  into a media article or institutional research note.

## Good Patterns

- Start from the user's own observation:
  "Recently I have been organizing AI server names, and I feel like not
  everything should be stuffed into one phrase: GPU spillover."
- Separate branches, but label them as hypotheses:
  "CPU is the line I can understand more clearly right now. HBM, cooling, and
  passive components do not feel like the exact same story."
- Separate evidence from opinion:
  "AMD is the cleanest source-backed line. Intel is more of a repair line.
  MediaTek is still optionality until official sources are stronger."
- Say what would change the view:
  "If the next earnings call does not confirm server CPU demand, this thesis
  should be marked down."

## Avoid

- Grand market claims without a source:
  "The market has started to..."
- Generic AI transitions:
  "This is important because..."
- Over-polished research terms:
  "core thesis", "underlying reason", "repricing", "reader takeaway".
- Investment-call language:
  "This is a buy", "the next winner is", "must own".
- Over-symmetrical article structure that feels like a model output.
- Forced analogies. Use them only when they make the technical point easier.

## Article Rhythm

1. Start with "what I have recently been organizing / observing".
2. Admit that the map is not verified yet.
3. Split the branches: for each one, say "how I currently think about it" and
   "what still needs verification".
4. Then add source-backed material to show which lines are stronger.
5. End with a watchlist or next research step, not a trade command.

## Public-Readiness Rule

Before public publishing, every strong claim must be tagged as one of:

- `source-backed`: supported by primary source or reliable official material.
- `secondary`: useful but not yet public-ready without confirmation.
- `my-interpretation`: clearly framed as judgment, not fact.
